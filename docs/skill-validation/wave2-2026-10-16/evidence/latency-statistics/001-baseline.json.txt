A statistically precise estimate of a coarse or\n   truncated histogram is still wrong.\n6. **Aggregate mergeable distributions before querying.** Add compatible histogram counts or\n   raw observations, then compute the quantile. Record `n`, the\n  estimator/interpolation rule, and an interval or rank bounds; small `n` alone does not make it\n  undefined. With zero observations, report no estimated quantile. Two displayed p99 values are\ndescriptive estimates; a causal or release decision also needs a defined contrast, independent\nexperimental units, uncertainty and a practical threshold.\n\n## Define the question before choosing a test\n\nThese are different estimands:\n\n- `Q_B(.99) − Q_A(.99)` for the same request population;\n- ratio `Q_B(.99) / Q_A(.99)` when proportional change is meaningful;\n- `P(T > 300 ms)` or SLO error-budget consumption;\n- mean service demand or total latency cost;\n- p99 of a **typical process run** versus p99 of all pooled requests;\n- non-inferiority: “rule out a regression worse than +15 ms”, not merely “reject equality”.\n\nFix route/mix, outcome and timeout policy, state (cold/ramp/sustained), load, quantile definition\nand observation window. Write the smallest operationally relevant effect and asymmetric error\ncosts before seeing the result.\n\n## When traffic mix changes\n\nRoute, region or payload mix can change a fleet quantile even if every cohort's distribution is\nunchanged. For the observed user-experience SLI, retain the actual request weights; a mix-driven\nregression is still a change in that SLI. For a comparison at a common population mix, inspect\nwithin-cohort distributions and use declared common weights instead of attributing the fleet\ndifference directly to the build.\n\nFor treatment `t` and cohorts `c`, construct `F_t*(x) = sum_c w_c F_t,c(x)`, with nonnegative\nweights summing to one, then query its quantile. Normalize each cohort's empirical CDF or compatible\nhistogram by its own count before weighting; averaging cohort p99s does not implement this formula.\nState the target weights and preserve the experimental design in uncertainty calculations.\nIf a positive-weight cohort has no observations in one arm, its distribution is unavailable;\ncollect evidence or label any extrapolation model rather than assigning it zero latency.\n\nIllustrative finite sample: 9,950 requests at 10 ms and 50 at 500 ms have type-1 p99 = 10 ms.\nChanging only those counts to 9,800 and 200 makes p99 = 500 ms. The actual fraction at most\n300 ms falls from 99.5% to 98%. At common 98%/2% cohort weights, both distributions have p99 =\n500 ms; the weighted average of cohort p99s, 19.8 ms, is neither mixture's p99.\n\nReport actual-mix and common-mix results separately. Choose comparison cohorts from suitable\npre-treatment characteristics; silently conditioning on successful outcomes or a treatment-induced\nrouting change can remove part of the effect being studied. Standardization alone does not remove\nother confounding or establish causality.\n\n## Find the experimental unit\n\nRequests are nested in connections, JVMs, hosts, shards, zones and time periods. A million\nrequests from one JVM run provide fine within-run resolution but do not independently replicate\nJIT history, placement, cache state or day-level load. The unit is the smallest entity independently\nassigned to A or B; the analysis must preserve clustering above it.\n\n| Assignment/design                                  | What can be estimated                                     | Main threats                                                                   |\n| -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |\n| Randomise eligible requests within a process/fleet | request-population contrast under that shared environment | treatment interference, cache pollution, sticky sessions, unequal outcome loss |\n| Randomise JVM/pod instances                        | instance-level treatment effect                           | few clusters, routing/shard imbalance, shared downstream interference          |\n| Block whole A/B runs by host/time                  | sustained benchmark contrast                              | carry-over, warm state, order/period effects                                   |\n| Before/after deploy                                | observational temporal contrast                           | traffic, restart, host and dependency changes are confounded with build        |\n| One incident window per side                       | descriptive contrast                                      | no independent replication of the event; resampling cannot manufacture it      |\n\nRandomise assignment where possible; block known nuisance dimensions; restart/reset both arms\nunder the same policy; preserve all planned runs. Deterministic ABAB is only one blocking scheme\nand aliases with periodic drift. Pilot data estimates variance for sample-size planning; no fixed\n“five runs per side” rule is statistically universal.\n\n## Quantile resolution and rank uncertainty\n\nA sample quantile exists for any nonempty sample under a declared convention. Near the sample\nedge it changes in large steps and estimates the population quantile imprecisely. For a continuous\npopulation, `n(1−p)` is the expected number strictly beyond its p-quantile; atoms/ties can make that\ncount smaller. This is a resolution warning, not a theorem declaring a result defined at 10 and\npublishable at 100.\n\nFor independent identically distributed observations, binomial order-statistic intervals provide\ndistribution-free rank bounds for a population quantile. With dependence, replace `n` by neither a\nguessed “effective n” nor raw request count: preserve dependence through experimental replication,\ncluster/block methods, or a justified time-series model. Quantile definitions and histogram\ninterpolation add separate value-scale uncertainty.\n\n## Choose an analysis that matches the design\n\n### Randomised or paired experiment\n\nEstimate the predeclared contrast directly. For paired host/time blocks, aggregate within-block\nquantile or threshold-fraction differences only when the target is that run-level contrast; name\nits summary and weighting (for example, the mean paired difference with equal block weights).\nFor a pooled-request quantile contrast, recompute each arm's quantile and their difference within\neach design-respecting resample, preserving pairing and the target population's weights. Averaging\nblock quantile differences does not estimate this pooled contrast and can even reverse its sign.\nFor many randomised requests, a cluster-aware quantile method or hierarchical bootstrap can retain\nconnection/process/time clustering. A randomisation/permutation test should permute at the level\nwhere treatment was assigned, not individual requests when pods were assigned.\n\n### Replicated whole runs\n\nUse a hierarchical design when run/JVM variance matters:\n\n1. Decide whether the estimand is the typical-run quantile or the pooled-request quantile. They\n   differ when run volumes/distributions differ.\n2. Randomise/block run order and capture host, JVM, dataset, load and state.\n3. Estimate run-level and within-run variation from a pilot; allocate repetition where variance\n   lives, following Kalibera–Jones rather than treating requests as replicate JVMs.\n4. Bootstrap/resample at every sampled hierarchy level or fit a model whose assumptions and\n   diagnostics are explicit.\n5. Report the treatment contrast interval against both zero and the practical threshold.\n\nTaking a median of per-run p99s estimates a typical-run summary; pooling histograms estimates the\nrequest-weighted mixture. Neither is universally correct. Name the target population.\n\n### One time series/window per arm\n\nA moving/block bootstrap may estimate within-window sampling variation when the process is\napproximately stationary and blocks exceed relevant dependence. Select block length using the\nautocorrelation/queue timescale and sensitivity analysis—not “about one second”. Resample\ncontiguous blocks and keep outcome counts. This does not address treatment confounding, long\nseasonality, one-off failover, or host-level variance; label the result descriptive.\n\nResample raw events when policy permits. HdrHistogram values are quantised counts, not “raw at full\nprecision”; resampling them preserves their representation error. Classic Prometheus buckets\nusually support threshold fractions and interval-censored approximations, not a raw-sample\nbootstrap. For a quantile extremely near the observed maximum, ordinary n-out-of-n bootstrap can\nbehave poorly; use order-statistic bounds, additional data, sensitivity analysis, or a justified\nextreme-value model instead of returning a spuriously precise interval.\n\n## What common tests actually answer\n\n| Method                         | Legitimate target                                                              | Common misuse                                                                               |\n| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |\n| Student/Welch t interval       | difference in means for independent units, with robustness/asymptotics checked | applied to request rows when the target is p99 or when one run is the true unit             |\n| Mann–Whitney/Wilcoxon rank-sum | probability-of-superiority/rank-distribution contrast under its assumptions    | called a median or tail test when shapes differ; request-level pseudoreplication            |\n| Kolmogorov–Smirnov             | supremum difference between two CDFs                                           | used to decide a specific p99/SLO effect; significance driven by an irrelevant region       |\n| Marginal confidence intervals  | uncertainty of A and B separately                                              | overlap/non-overlap treated as the interval or test for `B−A`                               |\n| Best-run/range comparison      | descriptive extrema of chosen runs                                             | called a confidence procedure; ranges expand with run count and have no fixed confidence    |\n| Bootstrap                      | sampling approximation under the resampling scheme                             | individual rows resampled despite clusters, nonstationarity, censoring or assignment by pod |\n\nA small p-value does not measure effect size, production value or probability that the null is\ntrue. A non-significant result does not establish equivalence. For a release gate, an interval\nentirely below the allowed regression margin is usually closer to the decision than a test of\nexact equality.\n\n## Timeouts, errors and censoring\n\nIf latency is recorded only for successful completions, a slower treatment may appear faster by\ntiming out its worst requests. Reconcile offered/admitted/terminal counts and report outcome rates.\nA timeout response's terminal latency is observed. For a different variable—time to an\neventual completion that remains unobserved—stopping observation at `d` gives a lower\nbound only if the operation was still unfinished then. Cancellation that prevents completion\nalso changes the outcome process; do not automatically treat it as independent censoring.\nKaplan–Meier or other survival methods require defensible censoring assumptions;\ndeadline censoring concentrated at one value cannot identify tail latency beyond that point\nwithout a model. Often the operational estimand should be the timeout/error fraction plus the\nsuccessful-latency distribution, not a fabricated completion time.\n\n## Reporting template\n\nIllustrative numbers only; this is not an executed experiment.\n\n```text\nDecision:       rule out a mean run-level p99 regression > +15 ms at 800 offered rps\nPopulation:     route=/pay, production-shaped mix; timeouts remain terminal outcomes\nDesign/unit:    8 paired host/time blocks; JVM run independently restarted; A/B order randomised\nRepresentation: raw monotonic durations; type-1 q(.99); timeout deadline 1 s\nEstimate:       mean run-level p99 A 212 ms, B 219 ms; mean paired Δp99 = +7 ms\nUncertainty:    95% interval for paired contrast [−2, +13] ms; hierarchy/block method attached\nGuardrails:     offered/completed/error counts, throughput, CPU and timeout rate unchanged\nThreats:        shared database interference; one region only\nDecision:       passes +15 ms non-inferiority margin;     "Content": "# Coordinated omission\n\n## Define the observation process first\n\nCoordinated omission occurs when the system being measured delays or suppresses future\nmeasurements that should have occurred under the target arrival schedule. The observed sample is\nthen conditioned on the measured system being responsive. A simple closed-loop worker that sends\nits next request only after the prior response is the canonical case.\n\nThe slow response is usually recorded. Missing are the arrivals—and therefore queue waits and\nterminal outcomes—that the target workload model says would have occurred while the worker was\nblocked. This can make latency look better and offered capacity look higher than under the intended\narrival process.\n\nNot every closed-loop test is invalid. It correctly models a population where each user waits for\nthe prior result plus think time. It is invalid when interpreted as a fixed/open arrival workload\nor when the production population contains enough independent users that the generator's finite\nworkers become the limiting feedback loop.\n\n## Evidence packet\n\nRecord counters at each stage over the same monotonic interval:\n\n```text\nscheduled/offered → admitted by generator → started on wire → accepted by service\n                  → completed / failed / timed out / cancelled\ngenerator workers/VUs, event-loop lag, CPU, sockets and queue depth\n```\n\nA scheduled-versus-started deficit proves that the generator missed its schedule; it does **not**\nalone prove coordinated omission. The cause may be generator CPU, connection limits, admission\npolicy or response-coupled workers. Correlate the deficit with worker state and response\ncompletion. Conversely, equal counts do not prove validity if timestamps are shifted, bursts are\ncompressed after stalls, or client-side queue delay is excluded from “latency”.\n\nAsk four questions:\n\n1. What arrival model is the production hypothesis—closed, open, trace replay, or stateful mix?\n2. Is issue time scheduled independently of prior completion?\n3. Where does the latency clock start—scheduled time, generator queue, socket write, server\n   receive, or handler entry?\n4. What happens when the generator cannot keep up—drop, queue, burst later, add workers, or reduce\n   rate?\n\nTools expose different signals. For k6 arrival-rate executors, inspect `dropped_iterations`, VU\nlimits and generator resource saturation; a nonzero value says the requested schedule was not\nrealised, not why. A closed-workload N-versus-2N run can reveal a concurrency/throughput knee, but\nlatency doubling with flat throughput is compatible with ordinary queue saturation and is not a\nconclusive omission test.\n\n## Prefer generation-time fidelity\n\nSchedule arrivals independently when that is the production model, preallocate enough generator\ncapacity, and include client-side waiting from scheduled arrival to terminal outcome.\n\n```javascript\nexport const options = {\n  scenarios: {\n    steady: {\n      executor: 'constant-arrival-rate',\n      rate: 500,\n      timeUnit: '1s',\n      duration: '10m',\n      preAllocatedVUs: 200,\n      maxVUs: 2000,\n    },\n  },\n};\n```\n\nThis configuration is a starting hypothesis, not proof: validate actual start timestamps,\n`dropped_iterations`, generator CPU/event-loop lag, sockets and terminal counts. Open-loop load can\noverwhelm a system exactly as production would; pair it with abort thresholds and a bounded blast\nradius.\n\n## HdrHistogram correction is a model, not recovered data\n\n```java\nhistogram.recordValueWithExpectedInterval(latencyNanos, expectedIntervalNanos);\n```\n\n`recordValueWithExpectedInterval` adds synthetic values at expected-interval steps below an\nobserved long latency. It answers a counterfactual resembling “what would a regularly scheduled\nsingle stream have observed during this stall?” It does not reconstruct actual arrival times,\nconcurrency, queue discipline, retries, drops or correlated service times. Results depend directly\non the chosen expected interval.\n\nUse it when only omission-prone samples remain and the regular-interval model is defensible.\nReport both uncorrected and corrected distributions, the interval and the model assumptions. Do\nnot apply correction again to measurements already scheduled independently, and do not merge\ncorrected and raw histograms as if they were the same population.\n\n## Distributed and asynchronous forms\n\n- A scheduled executor that suppresses/merges a run while the previous run is active measures\n  completed jobs, not intended trigger latency.\n- A consumer reporting handler time omits broker dwell and client prefetch queues; end-to-end age\n  needs producer timestamp semantics and clock/error handling.\n- A retry loop that starts its latency clock at each attempt omits backoff and failed attempts from\n  user-perceived completion time.\n- A circuit breaker or load shedder removes work from the success-latency histogram; those outcomes\n  belong in the denominator and usually in a separate terminal-outcome view.\n- A tracing sampler that preferentially retains errors/slow traces changes the observed\n  distribution; weighted reconstruction requires known inclusion probabilities.\n\n## Validation and failure injection\n\nInject a known pause or service-time step while arrivals remain scheduled. Verify that:\n\n- offered/start timestamps retain the intended cadence until an explicit bounded drop policy;\n- client queue time is included or separately reported;\n- mutually exclusive terminal outcomes plus still-in-flight work reconcile with admitted\n  work for the same arrival cohort; for a wall-clock window, also account for in-flight\n  work at its start and end (do not count a timeout again as a generic failure);\n- the histogram shows the expected queue/recovery shape rather than one long request only;\n- generator saturation alarms before the generator becomes the bottleneck.\n\n## Sources\n\n- [HdrHistogram coordinated-omission support](https://github.com/HdrHistogram/HdrHistogram#correcting-for-coordinated-omission)\n- [k6 arrival-rate executors](https://grafana.com/docs/k6/latest/using-k6/scenarios/concepts/arrival-rate-vu-allocation/)\n- [RFC 2330, Framework for IP Performance Metrics](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2330)\n- [Schroeder et al., “Open Versus Closed: A Cautionary Tale” (NSDI 2006)](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi-06/open-versus-closed-cautionary-tale)\n"

    "Content": "# Histograms, quantile resolution and aggregation\n\n## Start with the representation contract\n\nA histogram is a lossy distribution representation. Before trusting a derived quantile, record:\n\n| Property                                | Failure when omitted                                                                          |\n| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |\n| Value unit and clock endpoints          | nanoseconds merged as milliseconds; client and server durations compared as if identical      |\n| Lowest/highest trackable value          | underflow, clamping, exception, saturation or dropped observations masquerades as a good tail |\n| Bucket boundaries or exponential schema | interpolation error is larger than the regression being discussed                             |\n| Significant digits/resolution           | quantisation is mistaken for workload stability                                               |\n| Window and reset semantics              | cumulative lifetime data, rotating client windows and range-vector deltas are compared        |\n| Outcome policy                          | errors and timeouts disappear from the “successful request” distribution                      |\n| Library/backend/version                 | defaults and native-histogram support change independently of application code                |\n\nInspect overflow/error counters and reconcile histogram `_count` with the independently counted\nterminal outcomes. Unaccounted overflow, clamping or dropped observations invalidate deadline\ncompliance claims. A complete finite bucket at or below an inclusive deadline gives a conservative\nlower bound on the compliant fraction, which can suffice for a decision even without an exact\ndeadline bucket. A bucket spanning the deadline cannot resolve the exact compliant fraction.\n\n## Why averaging quantiles does not recover the union's quantile\n\nA quantile is nonlinear. Per-instance/window p99 values and their traffic weights do not contain\nenough information to recover the union's p99; averaging them can err in either direction. Merge\ncompatible observations or histogram counts first, then query the combined distribution.\n\n```java\n// Target range and precision must cover every source.\nHistogram fleet = new Histogram(highestTrackableNanos, 3);\nfleet.add(instanceA);\nfleet.add(instanceB);\nlong p99Nanos = fleet.getValueAtPercentile(99.0);\n```\n\n```promql\n# Classic cumulative buckets: rate first, preserve `le` while aggregating, quantile last.\nhistogram_quantile(\n  0.99,\n  sum by (le) (rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_bucket[5m]))\n)\n```\n\n```promql\n# Native histogram samples: no `le` label.\nhistogram_quantile(\n  0.99,\n  sum(rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds[5m]))\n)\n```\n\nFilter/group by dimensions required by the decision (`route`, `region`, outcome policy) before\nremoving labels. Do not merge populations with different clocks or semantics merely because their\nmetric names match.\n\n## Sample quantiles and resolution\n\nA sample quantile is an estimator based on order statistics. Multiple interpolation conventions\nexist (Hyndman–Fan enumerate nine), so small-sample results can differ across tools. For empirical\nCDF/type-1 semantics, p99 over 200 observations is order statistic `ceil(200 × .99) = 198`,\nthe third-largest when values are distinct: it is a\nvalid sample statistic, but a noisy estimate of a population tail.\n\nFor a continuous population, `n(1-p)` is the expected count strictly beyond its p-quantile;\natoms/ties can make that count smaller. Use it as a resolution warning, not a pass/fail threshold.\nReport:\n\n- total and terminal-outcome counts, plus the quantile convention;\n- distribution-free order-statistic rank bounds or an interval justified for the sampling design;\n- dependence and effective experimental unit, not only request count;\n- censoring/timeouts and the histogram's representation error.\n\nIf the required tail lies beyond available resolution, prefer a threshold fraction such as\n`P(T ≤ 300 ms)`, collect a longer valid window, pool exchangeable windows, or explicitly report\nthe bound. Do not rename a maximum or bucket edge “p99.99”.\n\n## HdrHistogram engineering\n\nHdrHistogram uses a fixed relative-precision bucket structure with bounded memory and effectively\nconstant-time recording over a configured range. Its quantisation is intentional; it does not\npreserve raw values.\n\n```java\nTimer timer = Timer.builder(\"http.server.duration\")\n    .publishPercentileHistogram()\n    .minimumExpectedValue(Duration.ofMillis(1))\n    .maximumExpectedValue(Duration.ofSeconds(30))\n    .register(registry);\n```\n\n- Three significant decimal digits provide roughly 0.1% value resolution across the configured\n  dynamic range; confirm the exact equivalent-value bounds for the library version.\n- Range, unit and precision jointly determine footprint. Use\n  `getEstimatedFootprintInBytes()` on the actual configuration rather than copying a universal\n  kilobyte figure.\n- Plain `Histogram` is unsynchronized: a single writer alone does not make concurrent reads,\n  merging or reset safe. Use `Recorder`, `SingleWriterRecorder` or a concurrent variant\n  according to ownership, and obtain stable interval snapshots without racing reset or reuse.\n- Adding can fail or lose the intended precision when the destination cannot represent a source\n  value/configuration. Pre-size the destination, test merge compatibility, and count failures;\n  auto-resize trades bounded memory for resilience.\n- Values beyond range, negative values and unit mistakes need tests at the instrumentation edge.\n\nMicrometer's `publishPercentiles(...)` exports client-computed quantiles with a local rotating\nwindow. Those values are not aggregable. `publishPercentileHistogram()` exports a mergeable\nhistogram where supported. Expiry, buffer length, bucket defaults and base units depend on meter,\nregistry and Micrometer version; inspect effective configuration rather than assuming “2 minutes”.\n\n## Classic, native and exponential histograms\n\n| Representation                       | Decision properties                                           | Migration/production hazards                                                                                    |\n| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |\n| Raw observations                     | Maximum analytical freedom                                    | high volume/privacy/storage; sampling can bias tails                                                            |\n| HdrHistogram                         | known range/relative precision; cheap local recording         | configured range, unit, concurrency and reset/merge ownership                                                   |\n| Prometheus classic histogram         | aggregable fixed cumulative buckets                           | one series per label-set/bucket; downscaling reduces resolution |\n| OpenTelemetry exponential histogram  | mergeable base-2 scales with downscaling                      | aggregation temporality, collector/backend translation and scale limits must be verified end-to-end             |\n\nFor classic histograms, place boundaries at inclusive decision thresholds (for example, at most\n300 ms) so the compliant fraction is a bucket-count ratio without quantile interpolation. Add\nboundaries around expected quantiles only when the storage/cardinality cost is justified. A rolling\ndeploy that changes classic bucket sets creates a mixed population whose cumulative buckets no longer\ndescribe one common ladder; dual-publish a new metric/schema or wait for convergence before\ncomparing.\n\nPrometheus native histograms became stable in Prometheus 3.8, but scraping remains explicitly\nconfigured in v3; the specification says scrape and remote-write defaults change in v4. Verify\nthe deployed server, ingestion protocol, remote-write receiver and query path. Standard schemas\ncan downscale to merge; custom-boundary native histograms generally require compatible boundaries.\n\n## Interpolation and error budget\n\n`histogram_quantile` estimates within the bucket containing the requested quantile. Classic\nhistograms generally interpolate linearly; standard native exponential buckets use exponential\ninterpolation for nonzero buckets. The result's resolution cannot be better than its bucket model.\nIf a proposed 10 ms improvement lies inside a 250 ms classic bucket, the dashboard cannot decide\nthe regression even when request count is enormous.\n\nCheck edge behavior before interpreting a plateau. For classic Prometheus histograms, a quantile\nin the `+Inf` bucket returns the last finite bucket's upper bound. If cumulative counts are 980 at\n30 s and 1,000 at `+Inf`, the displayed p99 is 30 s even though only 98% of observations were at\nmost 30 s. This identifies an unresolved tail above 30 s, not a measured p99 of 30 s. Extend the\nfinite range for future quantile estimates or obtain suitable raw observations; the existing\n30 s count fraction can still answer that threshold's compliance question.\n\nWith zero observations, `histogram_quantile` returns `NaN`. A missing `+Inf` bucket or fewer than\ntwo classic buckets also yields `NaN`; inspect counts, schema and query inputs before calling\nthat result “no traffic”. Preserve the distinction between no observations and broken telemetry.\n\nSeparate three uncertainties:\n\n1. **Sampling/process variation** — which requests, runs, hosts and time windows occurred.\n2. **Representation error** — quantisation, bucket interpolation, range truncation and export.\n3. **Causal uncertainty** — whether treatment rather than traffic, placement or restart caused\n   the difference.\n\nMore samples reduce only the first, and only under the sampling/dependence assumptions.\n\n## SLOs and alerting\n\nWhen the objective is “99% at most 300 ms”, use the `le=\"0.3\"` bucket fraction and burn rate,\nnot an interpolated p99 if avoidable. `le` includes observations exactly at the boundary. A strict\n`T < 300 ms` fraction needs evidence excluding or separately counting ties; the `T ≤ 300 ms`\nbucket alone does not generally identify that strict fraction. Include errors and timeouts in the SLI's\ndenominator according to its contract. Choose evaluation windows and `for`/burn-rate\nlogic from detection and recovery objectives; no universal five-minute rule exists. Validate\nqueries with synthetic boundary values, counter resets, absent series, mixed schemas and a known\nslow/error cohort.\n\n## Sources\n\n- [Prometheus: Histograms and summaries](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/histograms/)\n- [Prometheus histogram_quantile edge cases](https://prometheus.io/docs/prometheus/latest/querying/functions/#histogram_quantile) and [Prometheus 3.8 BucketQuantile implementation](https://github.com/prometheus/prometheus/blob/v3.8.0/promql/quantile.go) — empty/invalid histograms and the highest-bucket fallback.\n- [Prometheus native histogram specification](https://prometheus.io/docs/specs/native_histograms/)\n- [Micrometer: Histograms and percentiles](https://docs.micrometer.io/micrometer/reference/concepts/histogram-quantiles.html)\n- [HdrHistogram project and Java API notes](https://github.com/HdrHistogram/HdrHistogram)\n- [OpenTelemetry exponential histogram data model](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/otel/metrics/data-model/#exponentialhistogram)\n- [Hyndman and Fan, “Sample Quantiles in Statistical Packages” (1996)](https://robjhyndman.com/publications/quantiles/)\n"
